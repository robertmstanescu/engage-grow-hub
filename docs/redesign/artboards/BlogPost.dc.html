Who Signs Off When the Algorithm Sets the Pay?

241,000 drivers challenge Uber's pay algorithm under GDPR.

Robert Stănescu · 09 Sep 2026 · 6 min read

Worker Info Exchange International, acting through its Dutch foundation Stichting WIE International, has filed a collective legal claim against Uber at Amsterdam District Court, the city where Uber holds its EU legal seat. The claim, reported by Personnel Today and the Irish Examiner on 2 September 2026, argues that Uber's algorithmic system for setting driver pay and allocating jobs breaches Article 22 of the GDPR, the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing. The claim covers roughly 241,000 drivers across the UK, the Netherlands, Belgium, France, Germany, Poland and Romania.

This is not Worker Info Exchange's first case against Uber. Its director, James Farrar, brought the 2021 UK Supreme Court case that established Uber drivers as workers rather than self-employed contractors. This new claim looks at something different: the mechanics of the algorithm that decides what each driver earns and which jobs they see, separate from the employment-status question that organisation fought in 2021.

A Fine That Set the Tone

The claim arrives weeks after the Dutch Data Protection Authority fined Uber €825 million, reported in the same coverage, for automated driver deactivations carried out without adequate human review. I am treating that fine as background here rather than a story I have checked against the regulator's own release; it comes to me through the same press coverage as the pay claim, one hop from the DPA itself. Taken together, the two actions point at the same underlying practice: decisions that affect a driver's ability to earn a living, made by a system, with a human somewhere in the loop on paper and, the claimants argue, nowhere near it in practice.

Cristina-Georgiana Ioanitescu, General Secretary of the App Drivers & Couriers Union, is quoted in the coverage in support of the claim. Mohammed Shirwa, a 41-year-old Rotterdam-based driver, is quoted describing the effect on his own earnings.

The Numbers Worth Treating With Care

Worker Info Exchange has put figures on what it says drivers in the UK and Netherlands lose annually to the algorithm's pay-setting. I am deliberately not repeating those figures here as settled fact. Worker Info Exchange is a worker-advocacy organisation running a legal claim, and it has every institutional reason to make the scale of driver loss sound as large as the evidence allows. That does not make the figures wrong. It makes them claimant-supplied, not court-verified, and a reader should hold them at that distance until a court or a neutral audit says otherwise.

What is harder to wave away is the structural question underneath the numbers: whichever the true figure, the claim is that pay was set and jobs were allocated by a system operating without the human review Article 22 requires. That question does not go away even if every disputed number turns out to be generous to the claimants.

Why the Legal Seat Matters Beyond Uber

The reason this claim matters to anyone who has never opened Uber's app is jurisdictional. GDPR already binds every employer and platform operating in the EU and UK. A finding against Uber's pay and allocation system would not stay ring-fenced to ride-hailing. Any employer using an algorithm to set pay, allocate shifts or flag people for deactivation, including retail or hospitality businesses scoring shift allocation through software, would be operating under whatever standard for "meaningful human involvement" a European court sets here. The mechanism transfers even where the industry does not.

Consider a supermarket chain scoring shift allocation by an algorithm that weighs availability, past punctuality and till accuracy, or a hotel group running automated scheduling software that deprioritises staff who query their hours. Neither business would recognise itself in a story about ride-hailing apps. Both would sit squarely inside the same legal question if this claim succeeds: was there a human being who could have overridden the system's decision, and did that person actually do so.

The Question That Matters for Employers

Most employers I speak to answer questions about algorithmic tools by checking whether the system is compliant on paper: is there a human-in-the-loop clause in the vendor contract, is there a data protection impact assessment on file. That is the wrong first question. The right one is simpler and harder to fake: does an actual person look at the outcome, with the standing and the information to change it, before an employee experiences it? That question matters more than the paperwork, because paperwork can describe a human-in-the-loop process that does not meaningfully exist in practice, and the gap between the two is exactly where regulators and now claimants are starting to look.

What Thirty Agents Taught Me About That Line

Earlier in my career, in a quality and training role at a previous employer in financial services, I coached a call centre team of around thirty customer service agents through the usual toolkit: quality-assurance software, business intelligence dashboards, contact-centre analytics. I used that data to spot skill gaps agent by agent and to drive process change, including a change to how debt write-offs worked, letting agents write off more without needing a manager's sign-off first.

None of that data was there to make the call by itself. It flagged where a gap probably was; a person then decided what to do about it, whether that meant a coaching conversation, a change to one agent's targets, or, in the write-off case, a decision that a whole category of front-line judgement no longer needed escalating. The dashboard did the noticing. A human did the deciding. That distinction sounds small until it disappears, and its disappearance is precisely what Article 22 exists to stop, and precisely what Worker Info Exchange is arguing happened here.
